Role in the risk framework

The FHWA VAST-based PROTECT index combines Exposure, Sensitivity, Asset Value, and Adaptive Capacity with Criticality. Wildcat supplies the debris-flow Exposure inputs: for each road segment, culvert, and bridge, what is the modeled probability, volume, and combined hazard class of a post-fire debris flow reaching it? Debris flow is the one selected hazard where no mappable layer exists today; wildcat is how the gap gets filled with a defensible, USGS-standard method.

Inputs (DEM, burn severity, soils, vegetation)→ wildcat: stream network + hazard models→ Segments, outlets, basins with P, V, hazard class→ Intersect with roads and culverts→ Debris-flow Exposure scores→ Risk Index Tool

Why wildcat

  • It is the USGS operational standard. The same models behind the USGS post-fire debris-flow hazard portal, packaged as a reproducible open-source tool (GPL-3.0, code.usgs.gov/ghsc/lhp/wildcat).
  • It supports pre-fire planning. Burn severity and dNBR accept constant values, the standard hook for hypothetical-burn assessments over unburned watersheds.
  • Every run is reproducible. Each command writes a configuration record that exactly reproduces its outputs: clean provenance for the Resilience Improvement Plan appendix.
  • Complementary, not duplicative. WEPP models chronic post-fire erosion and sediment yield; RAMMS (sole-source) simulates site-scale runout. Wildcat is the network-scale screening layer between them.

What the PROTECT analysis looks like, end to end

Six stages take the current empty folder to a published hazard layer. Stages 1 and 2 are one-time setup; stages 3 through 5 are the modeling loop; stage 6 delivers into the index. The target is a reviewed debris-flow exposure layer in time for Workshop 1 (Nov. 2026).

1. Stand up the environment

Wildcat requires Python 3.11+ and depends on rasterio and fiona, which cannot coexist with arcpy's GDAL. It gets a dedicated wildcat conda environment (commands in debris-flow/environment.md), installed from the USGS GitLab package registry. The notebook detects the environment and degrades gracefully, so scoping cells run anywhere.

2. Acquire the static inputs once

Three basin-wide inputs are fire-independent and get downloaded once into debris-flow/data/raw/: the USGS 3DEP 10 m DEM (the models are calibrated at 10 m; finer lidar is deliberately not used), STATSGO soil KF-factors, and LANDFIRE existing vegetation type (which builds the water and development masks). The pfdf library bundled with wildcat ships acquisition helpers for each.

3. Validate on a real fire (S1)

Before trusting the tool for planning, run it in its native mode: the Caldor Fire (2021), which burned into the basin at Echo Summit and Christmas Valley, with its real perimeter and dNBR. USGS published an operational Caldor assessment, so our segments, likelihoods, and hazard classes can be compared directly against theirs. Agreement validates the setup; disagreement gets diagnosed before moving on.

4. Run the pre-fire planning scenario (S2, the deliverable)

Wildcat is a post-fire tool, but PROTECT needs hazard for fires that have not happened. Following USGS pre-fire assessment practice, the basin's watersheds are assessed under a hypothetical burn: constant moderate-or-high burn severity across each burn unit (likely HUC12 watersheds intersecting the highway corridors, one wildcat project per unit, batched from the notebook). The result is the answer to: if this watershed burns, which channels below it produce debris flows, how likely, and how big?

5. Apply Tahoe design storms, then climate-adjusted storms (S3)

Every likelihood is conditional on a 15-minute rainfall intensity. Instead of the generic USGS defaults, runs use the basin's own NOAA Atlas 14 statistics already extracted by the climate pipeline: 31.6, 49.6, and 63.8 mm/hr for the 2-, 10-, and 25-year storms (basin median). When the climate pipeline's sub-daily intensity scaling lands, the same runs repeat with mid-century intensities, making debris-flow exposure responsive to the climate horizons like every other hazard in the index.

6. Post-process into the Risk Index

The exported segments and basins (GeoJSON, EPSG:26910) intersect with the road network and the culvert and bridge inventories. Each asset picks up its debris-flow exposure inputs: the maximum combined hazard class of intersecting basins, the likelihood at the chosen design storm, and the summed upstream potential volume. Those columns are normalized into the Exposure sub-index exactly like the other hazards, the layer is loaded toward the packaged Hazards REST service, and the Debris Flow row in the data inventory flips from needed to available.

Worked example: a culvert on US-50 below Echo Summit

A culvert drains a steep, confined, forested catchment. S2 says: if that catchment burns at moderate severity, the channel above the culvert has a 55 percent debris-flow likelihood at the 25-year 15-minute storm, with a potential volume in the 10,000 to 100,000 m³ class: combined hazard high. The culvert inherits that exposure, which is weighted with its condition (Sensitivity), replacement cost (Asset Value), detour length (Adaptive Capacity), and the criticality of US-50 into its master risk score. Fuels treatments upstream lower the modeled severity, which lowers the score: the layer responds to management, not just topography.

How a wildcat assessment runs

Four commands, run from the CLI or the Python API (the notebook uses the Python API so parameters can be swept programmatically). Each assessment is a self-contained project folder under debris-flow/projects/, one per scenario, never committed.

initialize→ preprocess→ assess→ export 
projects/<scenario>/
├── configuration.py     all settings; created by initialize, edited per scenario
├── inputs/              small files here; large rasters stay in data/raw/ via absolute paths
├── preprocessed/        every input reprojected and clipped to the DEM grid + config record
├── assessment/          segments.geojson, outlets.geojson, basins.geojson + config record
└── exports/             GIS formats (Shapefile, GeoJSON) in a chosen CRS + config record

Behaviors that shape the design

  • The fire perimeter defines the domain. It is buffered (3 km default) and everything is clipped to that extent. The pre-fire scenario therefore supplies hypothetical burn footprints.
  • The DEM sets the grid. All inputs are reprojected to the DEM's CRS, resolution, and alignment. Allowed resolution is 6.5 to 11 m; the models are calibrated at about 10 m.
  • dNBR and severity accept constants. dnbr = 525 or severity = 3 applies a uniform value: the hypothetical-burn hook.
  • Stream network design is tunable. Delineation (min_area_km2, min_burned_area_km2) and filtering (min_slope, max_confinement, min_burn_ratio, max_area_km2) default to the USGS operational style; runs start from defaults.
  • Basin location is the slow step. locate_basins is disabled during parameter sweeps and enabled for deliverable runs.
  • Every command writes a config record (configuration.txt) that exactly reproduces its outputs.

Skeleton (Python API)

from wildcat import initialize, preprocess, assess, export

initialize(project)
preprocess(project, perimeter=..., dem=..., dnbr=..., kf=..., kf_field="KFFACT", evt=...)
assess(project, I15_mm_hr=[31.6, 49.6, 63.8], locate_basins=True)
export(project, format="GeoJSON", crs=26910)

15-minute design storms for the M1 model

The Staley 2017 M1 model conditions every likelihood on peak 15-minute rainfall intensity (I15). The chart shows Atlas 14 partial-duration-series 15-minute intensities at the eight basin design-storm points from the climate pipeline, with the basin median in navy and the USGS default assessment intensities as dashed reference lines.

Even the basin's 2-year storm (31.6 mm/hr) exceeds three of the four USGS default intensities (16, 20, 24, 40 mm/hr). Tahoe-specific design storms are not optional refinement: the defaults would materially understate rainfall, and therefore understate likelihoods. Proposed set for S2: the 2-, 10-, and 25-year basin medians, with per-point values available for corridor-specific work.

Source: NOAA Atlas 14 depth-duration-frequency extracted at eight basin points by the climate pipeline (climate/data/processed/atlas14_ddf.csv). Intensities are 15-minute depths times four. Atlas 15 (with climate adjustment) is tracked as a future swap-in on the Climate Data page.
