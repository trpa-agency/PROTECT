Role in the risk framework

The FHWA VAST-based PROTECT index combines Exposure, Sensitivity, Asset Value, and Adaptive Capacity with Criticality. Climate data drives Exposure: for each asset and hazard, how much does the climate stressor change under future scenarios? Sensitivity and Asset Value come from asset attributes (material, size, condition); Adaptive Capacity from network redundancy.

Sources (LOCA2, WRF, gridMET, Atlas 14)→ Pipeline: subset to basin + 10 km, clip, per-horizon metrics→ Ensemble median + 10th/90th→ Per-asset exposure scores→ Risk Index Tool

How climate data becomes a risk score

The climate products on this page never reach the Risk Index Tool as raw grids. They pass through five steps that turn projections into a defensible, per-asset exposure score:

1. From global models to the basin

CMIP6 global climate models are downscaled two ways: statistically (LOCA2, 6 km daily, the full bi-state study area) and dynamically (WRF, 9 km hourly, better storm physics). The pipeline pulls only the Tahoe window - the TRPA boundary plus a 10 km buffer that keeps the highway approach corridors (US-50, SR-89, SR-28, SR-207, SR-267, SR-431) inside the analysis.

2. From daily weather to planning metrics

156 years of simulated daily weather per model compress into a handful of decision-relevant numbers per grid cell: how much the extreme storm grows, how the hottest day shifts, how snowpack thins. Each is computed for three 30-year horizons against the 1981-2010 baseline, under a middle (SSP2-4.5) and a high (SSP3-7.0) emissions pathway.

3. From one model to an ensemble

No single climate model is trusted alone. Five models chosen for skill over California (the Cal-Adapt General Use Projections) run through every metric; the index uses the ensemble median, and the 10th-90th percentile spread travels alongside as the uncertainty band shown in charts.

4. From grid cells to assets

Every transportation asset in the inventory - each culvert, bridge, road segment, transit facility - samples the metric grids at its location. The result is a set of climate columns on the asset table: the same asset registry that carries condition, criticality, and sensitivity attributes.

5. From climate columns to the Exposure sub-index

Per hazard, the climate columns are normalized to a 0-1 exposure score and weighted into the master PROTECT index alongside Sensitivity, Asset Value, Adaptive Capacity, and Criticality. Exposure is where the future enters the index; the other components describe the asset as it is today.

Worked example: a culvert on SR-89

A 24-inch corrugated metal culvert was sized for the Atlas 14 design storm of its era - say a 25-year, 24-hour depth of about 4 inches. The LOCA2 ensemble says mid-century storms of that rank carry more water; the change factor scales the design depth up, and the culvert's design margin shrinks or disappears. That margin change, combined with the culvert's condition (from the jurisdiction inventories) and the criticality of the road it drains, is the culvert's risk score. Where WRF hourly intensity is available, the same logic sharpens from daily totals to the short bursts that actually overtop inlets.

Where it lands

  • Risk Index Tool: exposure scores color the map, filterable by hazard, scenario, and horizon.
  • Consultant analysis: the processed summary grids and tables (not raw downloads) feed the vulnerability assessment.
  • Workshops: Workshop 1 (Nov 2026) reviews exposure results; Workshop 2 (May 2027) prioritizes adaptation actions; findings roll into the 2027 Resilience Improvement Plan.

NOAA Atlas 14 design-storm depths

Precipitation depth-duration-frequency estimates for eight basin locations, from the NWS Precipitation Frequency Data Server (partial duration series, mean estimates). California points fall in Atlas 14 Volume 6, Nevada points in Volume 1. Depths in inches as published; millimeter conversion included for pipeline use.

Atlas 14 is stationary: it assumes the historical record represents the future. The pipeline plans a change-factor adjustment from the LOCA2 ensemble until non-stationary NOAA Atlas 15 coverage publishes for CA/NV (currently pilot only).

Key methodological decisions

Bi-state grid: LOCA2 6 km is primary

The finer LOCA2-Hybrid product built for California's Fifth Climate Change Assessment covers California only, which would leave the Nevada side of the basin (east shore, Incline Village, Kingsbury, Mt Rose corridor) unscored. Every asset therefore scores from the LOCA2 North America 1/16-degree (~6 km) product, pulled from the Cal-Adapt open S3 archive on a western-US domain that covers the full study area. LOCA2-Hybrid is retained as an optional CA-side sensitivity layer only. Contrary to common description it is the same 1/16-degree grid, not 3 km.

WRF scenario asymmetry

Dynamically downscaled WRF data (hourly precipitation intensity, snow water equivalent, wind) exists for historical and SSP3-7.0 only. Any WRF-derived metric shown beside two-scenario LOCA2 results carries this footnote.

Units and conversions

LOCA2 precipitation arrives as kg m-2 s-1 and is converted to mm/day (multiply by 86,400); temperatures arrive in Kelvin and are converted to degC. WRF precipitation is run-accumulated and is differenced to hourly rates. Automated QA asserts these conversions on every run.

Provenance and reproducibility

Every download is recorded in a manifest (source URL, size, checksum, retrieval date). Raw subsets stay on local disk and are excluded from the repository; only reviewed summary products are published. Known caveats

  • Statistical downscaling (LOCA2) mutes the most extreme tail of precipitation; WRF complements it dynamically for intensity.
  • gridMET is gauge-interpolated: sparse high-elevation stations add uncertainty to mountain precipitation and wind baselines.
  • Atlas 14 stationarity (see Design Storms tab).
  • WRF ensemble (selected CMIP6 GCMs) is smaller than the LOCA2 ensemble; wind and snow ensemble statistics are not directly comparable to precip/temp.
