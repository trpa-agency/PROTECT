How the numbers are made
  • Traffic: modeled 2024 average annual daily traffic, grouped into 10 natural-break (Jenks) classes. Major roads with no modeled value take the value of the connecting major road; local and service streets without one count as low traffic. Only the class is published here.
  • Detour length: the extra distance of the shortest detour when the segment is removed from the network (single link redundancy in RA2CE, on the corrected network), in 10 Jenks classes. Streets with no alternative at all, mostly dead ends and driveways, take the lowest class.
  • Access to critical services: residents of 2,201 hexagons routed by travel time to basin exits, law enforcement, medical facilities, emergency shelters, schools and fresh food locations, averaged over 100 routing runs with small random variation in travel time so near-equal routes share the trips. The mean across the six destination types is grouped into 10 Jenks classes.
  • Equity: for each segment, the highest count among the census block groups it crosses of households below the poverty line, residents who speak English less than well, households with no vehicle, and residents with a disability; the four are weighted equally by default.
  • Combining: each variable is normalized across all segments (z-score: distance from the mean in standard deviations; or min-max: scaled 0 to 1). A sub-index is the weighted sum of its variables, and the score is the weighted sum of the sub-indices. Weights are rebalanced to total 1 as a slider moves. Invert flips a variable so that lower values score higher.
  • Classes and colors: each segment is ranked against all 18,285 under the current weights and cut into quarters at the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles: criticality class 0 (lowest quarter) to 3 (top quarter), the same rule the Results page uses. Segments with the same score share the average rank of their group and stay in one class, so when one sub-index carries all the weight (its 1 to 10 classes tie on many segments) the tied majority lands in the class its rank puts it in, not at the top. Colors run navy (least critical) to gold (most critical); there is no red, because these are relative ranks, not hazard. A hazard filter changes what is shown, never the scores or the classes.
  • Hazards: FEMA flood zones (100- and 500-year), areas with a higher probability of high-severity wildfire, and the USGS landslide susceptibility mean along the segment. Hazard exposure is kept separate from criticality; the Results page reads the two together.
