Which street segments matter most to the basin's transportation system: how much traffic they carry, how far drivers would have to detour if they closed, how many residents depend on them to reach exits, emergency services, schools and food, and the needs of the neighborhoods around them.

Weights

The default weights are the method: traffic 0.3, detour length 0.3, access to critical services 0.3, and equity 0.1. Moving a slider is for testing; the map and table follow.

  • An evacuation planner might raise access, to weight exits and services.
  • A transit or highway agency might raise traffic.
  • An equity review might raise the neighborhood weight.

How the numbers are made
  • Traffic: modeled 2024 average annual daily traffic, grouped into 10 natural-break (Jenks) classes. Major roads with no modeled value take the value of the connecting major road; local and service streets without one count as low traffic. Only the class is published here, so the map's traffic view shows the volume class (1 lowest to 10 highest, grouped in four), not a quarter.
  • Detour length: the extra distance of the shortest detour when the segment is removed from the network (single link redundancy in RA2CE, on the corrected network), in 10 Jenks classes for the score. Streets with no alternative at all, mostly dead ends and driveways, take the lowest class. The map's detour view shows the distance itself, in miles.
  • Access to critical services: residents of 2,201 hexagons routed by travel time to basin exits, law enforcement, medical facilities, emergency shelters, schools, and fresh food locations, averaged over 100 routing runs with small random variation in travel time so near-equal routes share the trips. The mean across the six destination types is grouped into 10 Jenks classes for the score. The map's access view shows the mean itself, in residents routed.
  • Equity: for each segment, the highest count among the census block groups it crosses of households below the poverty line, residents who speak English less than well, households with no vehicle, and residents with a disability; the four are weighted equally by default.
  • Combining: each variable is converted to a z-score across all segments: its distance from the mean, in standard deviations.i A sub-index is the weighted sum of its variables, and the score is the weighted sum of the sub-indices. Weights are rebalanced to total 1 as a slider moves. Invert flips a variable so that lower values score higher.
  • Quarters and colors: each segment is ranked against every other under the current weights and cut into quarters at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles: criticality quarter 0 (lowest) to 3 (top), the same rule the Results page uses. When many segments share the same value they share a rank, so a quarter can hold more or fewer than a quarter of the segments. The traffic, detour length, and access views color the map by the input itself at fixed breaks (stated in each legend), because those inputs are class scores that sit at the lowest class on most segments, and rank quarters would collapse to two colors. Colors run dark (lowest) to bright (highest) on the Phosphor thermal ramp, drawn on a dark basemap so the most critical roads stand out. A hazard filter changes what is shown, never the scores or the quarters.
  • Hazards: FEMA flood zones (100- and 500-year), areas with a higher probability of high-severity wildfire, and the USGS landslide susceptibility mean along the segment. Hazard exposure is kept separate from criticality; the Results page reads the two together.
